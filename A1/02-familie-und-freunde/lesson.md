## 2. Einstieg — Was lernst du heute?

You already know how to say *Hallo*, where you come from, and how to count to 20. Now you meet the next big topic: **family**. In almost every German conversation, people talk about their families — how many siblings they have, whether they are married, how old their parents are. In this lesson you learn the verb *haben* (to have), how to use *mein/dein/sein/ihr* (my/your/his/her) to show who belongs to whom, how to count from 20 to 1000, and how to say "yes" and "no" to a direct question. By the end you will be able to look at a photo and say something real about every person in it.

## 4. Wortschatz (vocabulary)

### Die Familie — always learn nouns with article AND plural!

> 💡 **Lerntipp:** German nouns have a grammatical gender — masculine (*der*), feminine (*die*), or neuter (*das*) — and you have to learn the gender together with the noun. There is no shortcut. For family words, notice the pattern: male family members are almost always *der*, female family members *die*. Start with the most common pairs: *der Vater / die Mutter*, *der Bruder / die Schwester*, *der Sohn / die Tochter*. Learn them in pairs, not in isolation.

| Maskulin | Feminin | Plural / group |
|---|---|---|
| der Vater, – (Väter) | die Mutter, – (Mütter) | die Eltern (only pl.) |
| der Bruder, – (Brüder) | die Schwester, -n | die Geschwister (siblings) |
| der Sohn, –e (Söhne) | die Tochter, – (Töchter) | die Kinder |
| der Mann, –er (husband/man) | die Frau, -en (wife/woman) | das Ehepaar (married couple) |
| der Opa, -s / der Großvater | die Oma, -s / die Großmutter | die Großeltern |
| der Onkel, - | die Tante, -n | |
| der Cousin, -s | die Cousine, -n | |
| der Enkel, - | die Enkelin, -nen | die Enkelkinder |
| der Freund, -e (friend/boyfriend) | die Freundin, -nen | die Freunde |
| das Kind, -er · das Baby, -s · die Familie, -n · das Foto, -s | | |

– means umlaut in the plural: der Vater → die V**ä**ter.

### Die Zahlen 20–1000

> 💡 **Lerntipp:** The big rule for German numbers 21–99 is that units come before tens — the opposite of English. *Einundzwanzig* is literally "one-and-twenty". Say it out loud once: *ein-und-zwan-zig*. You only need to memorise the "decade" numbers (zwanzig, dreißig … neunzig), and the units 1–9 you already know. Everything in between follows the same backwards pattern, written as one word.

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| 20 zwanzig | 21 **einundzwanzig** | 22 zweiundzwanzig |
| 30 **dreißig** ⚠️ | 40 vierzig | 50 fünfzig |
| 60 **sechzig** ⚠️ | 70 **siebzig** ⚠️ | 80 achtzig |
| 90 neunzig | 100 (ein)hundert | 1000 (ein)tausend |

**The famous backwards rule:** units come first! 21 = *ein-und-zwanzig*
("one-and-twenty"), 47 = *siebenundvierzig*, 99 = *neunundneunzig*.
One word, no spaces, however long: 358 = dreihundertachtundfünfzig.

⚠️ Irregular: drei**ßig** (ßig, not zig), sech**zig** (no s), sieb**zig** (no en).
And 21/31/41… use **ein**, not eins: einundzwanzig.

### Useful adjectives (predicative, from Lektion 1's pattern)

> 💡 **Lerntipp:** These adjectives come *after* the verb *sein* (predicative position) — you already know this pattern from *Lektion 1*: *Er ist nett. Sie ist groß.* At this stage you do not need to worry about adjective endings before a noun — that comes in Lektion 4. For now, use them after *sein* and you can describe any family member.

groß (big/tall) · klein (small) · alt (old) · jung (young) · nett (nice) ·
fit (fit) · süß (sweet/cute) · toll (great)

## 6. Grammatik

### 6.1 The verb `haben` (to have) — irregular, memorize it

**Warum?** *Haben* (to have) is the second most important verb in German after *sein*. You need it immediately — to say what family members you have, what languages you have, even to form past tenses later (you'll do this in Lektion 11). The bad news: it's irregular in two slots, just like its English cousin *have → has*. The good news: it's only two slots, and the rest of the table follows the normal pattern you already know from *kommen* and *wohnen*. The two irregular forms to nail right now are *du **hast*** and *er/sie/es **hat*** — notice how the stem loses its *-b-* in those slots. In Portuguese and Spanish you would say *você tem*, in French *tu as* — German *du hast* is somewhere between them. Think: *hast* sounds like "hast" in "hasten" — fast.

| | haben |
|---|---|
| ich | **habe** |
| du | **hast** ⚠️ |
| er/sie/es | **hat** ⚠️ |
| wir | **haben** |
| ihr | **habt** |
| sie/Sie | **haben** |

**Examples:**

1. *Ich **habe** einen Bruder.* · 2. *Du **hast** Geschwister?* · 3. *Er **hat** eine Tochter.* · 4. *Sie **hat** zwei Söhne.* · 5. *Anna **hat** einen Bruder und eine Schwester.* · 6. *Wir **haben** ein Kind.* · 7. *Ihr **habt** keine Eltern hier?* · 8. *Sie **haben** drei Kinder.* · 9. *Bruno **hat** eine Nichte.* · 10. *Das Baby **hat** eine Oma in Wien.* · 11. *Meine Eltern **haben** einen Hund.* · 12. *Hast du ein Foto von deiner Familie?*

> ⚠️ **Häufige Fehler**
>
> | ❌ Wrong | ✅ Right | Why |
> |---|---|---|
> | *Ich **habe** zwanzig Jahre.* | *Ich **bin** zwanzig Jahre alt.* | German uses *sein* for age, not *haben*. Portuguese (*tenho*) and Spanish (*tengo*) use "have" for age; German does not. |
> | *Er **haben** zwei Söhne.* | *Er **hat** zwei Söhne.* | After *er/sie/es* the verb drops the *-b-* and takes *-t*. Never the infinitive form. |
> | *Du **habe** Geschwister?* | *Du **hast** Geschwister?* | After *du* the ending is *-st*, not *-e*. *hast* not *habe*. |
> | *Anna **hab** einen Bruder.* | *Anna **hat** einen Bruder.* | *Anna* = 3rd person singular = *hat*. Bare stem without an ending is never correct. |

> 💡 **Lerntipp:** The whole table hinges on two irregular forms: *du **hast*** and *er/sie/es **hat***. Write them on a sticky note and put it on your bathroom mirror: *ich habe — du HAST — er HAT*. The rest of the table (*wir haben, ihr habt, sie haben*) follows the regular pattern you already know. Once *hast* and *hat* are automatic, the whole verb is yours.

**Versuch es selbst.** Write 4 sentences using *haben*: one with *ich*, one with *du* (question form), one with *er* or *sie*, and one with *wir*. Use family vocabulary from §4. Example start: *Ich habe …*

<summary>📝 Lösung</summary>

> *Ich **habe** eine Schwester und einen Bruder.*
> *Hast du Geschwister?*
> *Er **hat** zwei Töchter.*
> *Wir **haben** keine Kinder.*
>
> Beachte: *habe → hast → hat* — the stem loses its *b* in the du and er/sie/es slots.

</details>

> 📌 **Merkasten — haben**
>
> | Pronomen | haben |
> |---|---|
> | ich | **habe** |
> | du | **hast** |
> | er · sie · es | **hat** |
> | wir | **haben** |

> | sie · Sie | **haben** |
>
> ▶ Irregular: the stems shift (*hab- → has- → hat-*). Memorize *du **hast*** and *er **hat*** — they are the tricky ones. Everything else follows regular endings.

### 6.2 Possessive articles (Nominativ) — mein, dein, sein, ihr…

**Warum?** English has one word "my", one word "your", one word "his". German uses those words too — *mein, dein, sein* — but they do something extra: they change their endings depending on the noun that follows. Think of it this way: the *stem* of the possessive tells you the *owner* (*mein-* = I own it, *sein-* = he owns it), and the *ending* tells you what kind of noun comes next (masculine/neuter → no ending, feminine/plural → add *-e*). In Portuguese you have *meu/minha*, in Spanish *mi/mis* — the German system is similar but slightly more detailed because it also tracks neuter. The pattern mirrors *ein/eine/ein* exactly: if you can say *ein Vater, eine Mutter, ein Kind*, you can already use *mein* correctly — it's the same three endings.

| owner | before masc./neuter noun | before fem./plural noun |
|---|---|---|
| ich | **mein** Vater / Kind | **meine** Mutter / Eltern |
| du | **dein** Bruder | **deine** Schwester |
| er/es | **sein** Sohn (his) | **seine** Tochter (his) |
| sie | **ihr** Sohn (her) | **ihre** Tochter (her) |
| wir | **unser** Opa | **unsere** Oma |
| ihr | **euer** Opa ⚠️ | **eure** Oma ⚠️ |
| sie | **ihr** Kind (their) | **ihre** Kinder (their) |
| Sie | **Ihr** Mann (your, formal) | **Ihre** Frau (your, formal) |

Two rules:
1. The **ending** follows the noun that comes after: feminine or plural noun → add `-e`.
2. The **stem** follows the owner. Classic trap: `sein` = *his*, `ihr` = *her*.
   > Das ist Bruno. **Seine** Frau heißt Paula. (his wife)
   > Das ist Anna. **Ihr** Bruder heißt Felix. (her brother)

⚠️ `euer` drops the inner -e- when it gets an ending: euer Vater, but **eure** Mutter.

**Examples:**

1. *Das ist **mein** Bruder.* · 2. *Das ist **meine** Schwester.* · 3. ***Sein** Vater heißt Roberto.* · 4. ***Ihre** Mutter heißt Claudia.* · 5. ***Dein** Sohn ist sehr süß!* · 6. ***Unsere** Oma wohnt in Wien.* · 7. *Das sind **meine** Eltern.* · 8. *Bruno zeigt **sein** Foto.* · 9. ***Ihr** Kind heißt Sofia.* (her child) · 10. ***Ihre** Kinder sind jung.* (their children) · 11. *Herr Steinmeyer, ist das **Ihre** Frau?* · 12. *Anna hat **ihren** Bruder gern.* (chunk — accusative, Lektion 3)

> ⚠️ **Häufige Fehler**
>
> | ❌ Wrong | ✅ Right | Why |
> |---|---|---|
> | *Das ist **sein** Frau.* | *Das ist **seine** Frau.* | *Frau* is feminine → add *-e*: *seine*. |
> | *Das ist **ihr** Bruder* (for "his brother") | *Das ist **sein** Bruder.* | *sein* = his, *ihr* = her. The stems encode the owner's gender. |
> | *Das ist **mein** Mutter.* | *Das ist **meine** Mutter.* | *Mutter* is feminine → the ending must be *-e*: *meine*. |
> | *Das sind **euer** Kinder.* | *Das sind **eure** Kinder.* | Before a plural noun, always add *-e*: *eure*. And *euer* drops the inner *e*. |

> 💡 **Lerntipp:** The possessives follow exactly the same ending pattern as *ein/eine/ein*. So if you know *ein Vater, eine Mutter, ein Kind*, you also know *mein Vater, meine Mutter, mein Kind* — just swap *ein* → *mein*. The same trick works for *dein, sein, ihr, unser, ihr, Ihr*. There is literally no new ending to learn: it's the *ein*-pattern with different stems.

>
> ▶ Key: the endings mirror **ein/eine/ein** — add *-e* before feminine and plural nouns, no ending before masculine and neuter.

### 6.3 Plural of nouns — the five patterns

**Warum?** German noun plurals are famously hard — there is no single rule. In English, 99% of nouns add *-s* (*cat → cats, book → books*). In German, five different patterns exist and there is no reliable way to predict which pattern a noun takes from its form alone. The only way to handle this is to **always learn the plural together with the noun** — as a trio: article, singular, plural (*die Schwester, -n*). The good news: the five patterns cover almost all nouns, and once you know your family nouns cold, you've internalised the most common patterns. And remember: in the plural, the article is **always *die***, regardless of the singular gender.

| Pattern | Examples |
|---|---|
| **-(e)n** | die Schwester → Schwester**n**, die Frau → Frau**en**, die Tante → Tante**n** |
| **-e** (often + umlaut) | der Freund → Freund**e**, der Sohn → S**ö**hn**e** |
| **-er** (often + umlaut) | das Kind → Kind**er**, der Mann → M**ä**nn**er** |
| **-s** | das Baby → Baby**s**, der Opa → Opa**s**, das Foto → Foto**s** |
| **–** (often + umlaut) | der Bruder → Br**ü**der, die Mutter → M**ü**tter, der Onkel → Onkel |

**Examples:**

1. *Das Kind → die Kinder.* · 2. *Der Bruder → die Brüder.* · 3. *Die Schwester → die Schwestern.* · 4. *Die Mutter → die Mütter.* · 5. *Das Baby → die Babys.* · 6. *Der Sohn → die Söhne.* · 7. *Die Tochter → die Töchter.* · 8. *Die Frau → die Frauen.* · 9. *Der Vater → die Väter.* · 10. *Der Onkel → die Onkel.* · 11. *Die Cousine → die Cousinen.* · 12. *Der Mann → die Männer.*

> ⚠️ **Häufige Fehler**
>
> | ❌ Wrong | ✅ Right | Why |
> |---|---|---|
> | *die **Kinders*** | *die **Kinder*** | *Kind* takes *-er*, not *-s*. No foreign-language analogy. |
> | *die **Söhns*** | *die **Söhne*** | *Sohn* takes *-e* + umlaut. |
> | *die **Bruders*** | *die **Brüder*** | *Bruder* has no ending but takes an umlaut (*ü*). |
> | *die **Foto**s* → correct, but *die **Fotos*** not *die **Foten*** | *die Fotos* | Loanwords from English/Italian often take *-s*. Don't add *-en* here. |

> 💡 **Lerntipp:** When you write a new noun in your vocabulary notebook, always write it as a **trio** right away: *die Schwester, -n · der Sohn, –e (Söhne) · das Kind, -er*. The dash before the ending means "take the singular and add this ending". A umlaut dot over the dash means "also umlaut the stem vowel". This looks like a code, but it's the code used in every German dictionary — learn to read it now and every future vocab list becomes self-explanatory.

> 📌 **Merkasten — Plural of nouns**
>
> | Pattern | Example |
> |---|---|
> | **–** (umlaut, no ending) | der Bruder → die Brüder |
> | **-e** (often + umlaut) | der Sohn → die Söhne |

### 6.4 Yes/no questions — verb first

**Warum?** In Lektion 1 you learned the V2 rule: the conjugated verb is always in position 2. Yes/no questions are the one exception to this pattern — or rather, they follow a stricter version: the verb jumps all the way to **position 1**. There is no question word to fill position 1 (that would make it a W-question). The verb alone opens the sentence, and the subject follows. Compare English: *You are married → Are you married?* — English does the same inversion. German does it too, just consistently for all verbs, not just *to be* and *to have*. Once you have the V2 rule and the verb-first rule, you have the complete German question system at A1.

| Statement (verb 2nd) | Yes/no question (verb 1st) |
|---|---|
| Du **hast** Geschwister. | **Hast** du Geschwister? |
| Anna **ist** verheiratet. | **Ist** Anna verheiratet? |
| Ihr **habt** Kinder. | **Habt** ihr Kinder? |
| Bruno **kommt** aus Brasilien. | **Kommt** Bruno aus Brasilien? |

Answers: **Ja, …** / **Nein, …**
> Bist du verheiratet? — **Nein**, ich bin ledig.

**Examples:**

1. *Hast du Geschwister?* · 2. *Ist das deine Mutter?* · 3. *Habt ihr Kinder?* · 4. *Wohnen sie in Berlin?* · 5. *Sind Sie verheiratet?* · 6. *Kommt Anna aus Russland?* · 7. *Ist Felix zweiundzwanzig?* · 8. *Hat Frau Weber Söhne?* · 9. *Sprichst du Deutsch?* · 10. *Haben Sie ein Foto?* · 11. *Ist Sofia vier Jahre alt?* · 12. *Heißt deine Schwester Lena?*

> ⚠️ **Häufige Fehler**
>
> | ❌ Wrong | ✅ Right | Why |
> |---|---|---|
> | *Du hast Geschwister?* (rising intonation only) | ***Hast** du Geschwister?* | Intonation alone is not enough for a formal yes/no question — move the verb to position 1. |
> | ***Hast** Geschwister du?* | ***Hast** du Geschwister?* | Subject (*du*) must follow directly after the verb. |
> | *Ist Anna verheiratet **nicht**?* | *Ist Anna **nicht** verheiratet?* | Negation (*nicht*) belongs before the adjective, not at the end. |
> | *Du **bist** verheiratet?* → acceptable in very informal speech but not exam German | ***Bist** du verheiratet?* | Verb-first is the grammatically correct form for yes/no questions at A1. |

> 💡 **Lerntipp:** Yes/no questions in German work the same way as in English for *to be* and *to have*: *Du bist … → Bist du …?* · *Du hast … → Hast du …?* The difference is that German does it for **every** verb, not just *be* and *have*. So once you have the pattern, it's actually simpler than English. Write 5 statements from Lektion 1 or 2, then flip each one into a yes/no question by moving the verb to the front.

**Versuch es selbst.** Flip these statements into yes/no questions: 1. *Anna ist ledig.* 2. *Du hast drei Geschwister.* 3. *Herr Steinmeyer kommt aus Österreich.*

<details>
<summary>📝 Lösung</summary>

> 1. ***Ist** Anna ledig?*
> 2. ***Hast** du drei Geschwister?*
> 3. ***Kommt** Herr Steinmeyer aus Österreich?*
>
> Muster: Nimm das konjugierte Verb (*ist, hast, kommt*), stell es an Position 1, das Subjekt folgt auf Position 2.

</details>

> 📌 **Merkasten — Yes/no questions**
>
> Rule: verb moves to **position 1**, subject moves to **position 2**.
>
> | Statement (verb 2nd) | Yes/no question (verb 1st) |
> |---|---|
> | Du **hast** Geschwister. | **Hast** du Geschwister? |
>
> ▶ Invert verb and subject — no extra question word needed. This works for **all** German verbs, not only *sein* and *haben*.

### 6.5 Negation with `nicht`

**Warum?** Saying "no" or "not" in German has two tools: *nicht* and *kein*. This lesson introduces *nicht* — the all-purpose negator for verbs, adjectives, adverbs, and entire statements. *Kein* negates nouns that use an indefinite article (*ein Bruder → kein Bruder*), but that belongs to Lektion 3. For now, the rule is simple: *nicht* goes **before the word it negates** if that word is an adjective or a specific phrase. If you are negating the whole predicate (the whole action), *nicht* goes **after the verb and any object**. In English, "not" usually comes right after the auxiliary (*she is **not** married*, *he does **not** have*). German *nicht* floats a little more freely, but the positions below cover 90% of A1 needs.

Two safe positions:
- **Before an adjective or a noun-phrase being negated:**
  > Ich bin **nicht** verheiratet. · Das ist **nicht** meine Schwester.
- **After the verb (+ object) when negating the whole statement:**
  > Mein Bruder wohnt **nicht** in Hamburg. · Ich komme **nicht** aus Spanien.

(To negate a noun with *ein* — "no/not a" — German uses `kein`: that's Lektion 3.)

**Examples:**

1. *Ich bin **nicht** verheiratet.* · 2. *Das ist **nicht** mein Bruder.* · 3. *Anna wohnt **nicht** in München.* · 4. *Meine Mutter ist **nicht** alt.* · 5. *Bruno kommt **nicht** aus Portugal — er kommt aus Brasilien.* · 6. *Ich spreche **nicht** gut Deutsch.* · 7. *Das Foto ist **nicht** schön.* · 8. *Frau Weber ist **nicht** verheiratet — sie ist geschieden.* · 9. *Mein Opa ist **nicht** klein.* · 10. *Wir wohnen **nicht** in Wien.* · 11. *Das ist **nicht** meine Familie.* · 12. *Ich bin **nicht** aus Deutschland.*

> ⚠️ **Häufige Fehler**
>
> | ❌ Wrong | ✅ Right | Why |
> |---|---|---|
> | *Ich **nicht bin** verheiratet.* | *Ich bin **nicht** verheiratet.* | *nicht* never goes before the conjugated verb. It goes before the adjective or after the verb. |
> | *Ich bin verheiratet **nicht**.* | *Ich bin **nicht** verheiratet.* | *nicht* before the adjective here, not at the end of the sentence. |
> | *Das ist **nicht** ein Bruder.* | *Das ist **kein** Bruder.* | To negate a noun with *ein*, use *kein*, not *nicht ein*. (Full explanation in Lektion 3.) |
> | *Mein Bruder **nicht wohnt** in Hamburg.* | *Mein Bruder wohnt **nicht** in Hamburg.* | The conjugated verb (*wohnt*) stays in position 2. *nicht* comes after it. |

> 💡 **Lerntipp:** A simple test for *nicht* placement: ask "what am I negating?" If you're negating an **adjective or predicate noun** (*verheiratet, groß, meine Schwester*), put *nicht* right before it. If you're negating the **whole action** (*wohnen, kommen, sprechen*), put *nicht* after the verb. When in doubt, put it at the end of the clause (before any adjective or adverb) — this is almost always correct at A1.

**Versuch es selbst.** Negate these sentences with *nicht*: 1. *Anna ist verheiratet.* 2. *Mein Bruder wohnt in Berlin.* 3. *Das ist meine Mutter.*

<details>
<summary>📝 Lösung</summary>

> 1. *Anna ist **nicht** verheiratet.*
> 2. *Mein Bruder wohnt **nicht** in Berlin.*
> 3. *Das ist **nicht** meine Mutter.*
>
> Muster: 1 — *nicht* vor dem Adjektiv (*verheiratet*). 2 — *nicht* nach dem Verb (*wohnt*) und dem Objekt, weil die ganze Aktion verneint wird. 3 — *nicht* vor der Nominalphrase (*meine Mutter*), die verneint wird.

</details>

> 📌 **Merkasten — Negation with nicht**
>
> | Rule | Example |
> |---|---|
> | `nicht` before adjective or adverb | Ich bin **nicht** verheiratet. |
> | `nicht` at end of clause (general negation) | Mein Bruder wohnt **nicht** in Hamburg. |
> | `kein-` to negate a noun (replaces ein/eine/ein) | Ich habe **keine** Geschwister. *(Lektion 3)* |
>
> ▶ Use `nicht` to negate verbs, adjectives, and adverbs; use `kein-` to negate nouns that take an indefinite article. The *kein* rule comes in full in Lektion 3.

### 6.6 Pronunciation

**Warum?** Two pronunciation traps stand out in this lesson. The first is final *-er*: in German, the *r* at the end of a syllable (or in the ending *-er*) is swallowed — it becomes a weak schwa-like sound, not the English *r*. So *Vater* sounds like "FAH-ta", not "FAH-ter". The second is the *ch* sound, which changes depending on the vowel before it. Both are common sources of confusion and worth a few minutes of deliberate practice.

- Final **-er** is a weak "a" sound [ɐ]: Vat**er**, Mutt**er**, Brud**er** ≈ "FAH-ta", "MUH-ta", "BROO-da". The *r* disappears.
- **ch** has two sounds: soft after e/i (*Geschw**i**ster, m**i**ch*) — the "ich-sound" (like a gentle "h" in English "huge"); hard after a/o/u (*To**ch**ter, a**ch**t*) — like Scottish "lo**ch**".
- Final **-ig** sounds like "-ich": zwanz**ig** ≈ "TSVAN-tsich" (standard German), not "-ik".
- **z** recap: zwanzig, zweiundzwanzig — always "ts", never English z.

> 💡 **Lerntipp:** For the *-er* sound, say "FAAH" and then add a very lazy "a" at the end — "FAAH-a" — and you have "Vater". Do the same with Mutter, Bruder, Schwester. For *ch*, remember the rule: **after a/o/u = hard** (like clearing your throat), **after e/i = soft** (like hissing "shhh" from the front of your mouth). *Tochter* = hard ch. *Geschwister* = soft ch.

## 9. Magazin — Landeskunde: Familienmodelle in Deutschland heute

The picture of a typical German family — married couple, two kids, house in the suburbs — is at least 30 years out of date. Today, nearly 40% of German households are single-person, one of Europe's highest rates. Later marriages, more divorces, and a generation that moves cities for work have all played a part.

> *„Wir haben uns das anders vorgestellt."* — a sentence you hear often from German grandparents.

That said, the extended family hasn't disappeared. In Turkish, Arabic, Polish, and Russian communities in Germany, multi-generational households remain common. Your German class likely mirrors this: half the room probably comes from cultures where family ties are strong. Talking about your family is the fastest way to sound real in German — use it.
